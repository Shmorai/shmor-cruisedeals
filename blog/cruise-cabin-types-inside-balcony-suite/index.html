Cruise cabin types explained: inside, oceanview, balcony and suite

The cabin you choose is often the biggest single factor in the price of a cruise, and the choice is not just about money. The right cabin depends on where you sail, how much time you will spend in it and who you are travelling with. Here is how the main cabin types compare and what to check before you book.

What are the main cruise cabin types?

Most ships offer four broad categories, though names and sizes vary by cruise line and by ship:

  • Inside cabin: no window. Usually the lowest fare on the ship.
  • Oceanview cabin: a window or porthole, sometimes partly obstructed by a lifeboat. Light and a view, but no outdoor space.
  • Balcony cabin: a private outdoor space with a door to step through, usually at a higher fare.
  • Suite: more space and, on many lines, extra perks. Fares are higher and what is included differs by line, so read the booking terms.

Cruise lines subdivide these further (for example guaranteed cabins, where the line picks your cabin), so compare what is actually included rather than relying on the category name.

Is an inside cabin a good idea?

An inside cabin suits travellers who treat the cabin as a place to sleep and spend their days on deck, in restaurants and ashore. It can leave more of the budget for excursions and drinks. The drawbacks are no natural light and no view, which some people find hard on longer sailings. If you are unsure, an oceanview cabin is a modest step up in light.

Is a balcony cabin worth it?

It depends on the itinerary. On scenic routes such as Alaska or the Norwegian fjords, a private balcony lets you watch the coastline in comfort. It also helps in colder weather if you want fresh air without sharing deck space. On a short Mediterranean hop where you are ashore most of the day, it may not earn its extra cost. In hot, humid regions, balconies are used less in the heat of the day, so think about when you would really use it.

Where on the ship should you book?

Location matters as much as category. Open the line's deck plan and check what is directly above and below your cabin: cabins under restaurants, theatres or the pool deck can be noisy, while cabins sandwiched between other cabin decks tend to be quieter. Midship cabins are generally steadier in rough seas than those at the very front or back, which suits anyone prone to seasickness. Also look at the walk to lifts and the places you will use most. If you have mobility needs, look for accessible cabins and ask the cruise line what is available.

How to compare cabin prices properly

Compare the total cost, not just the headline fare: taxes and port fees, gratuities or service charges, and any onboard credit or drinks packages. Offers differ by cruise line and sailing, and "fly-cruise" prices include flights while others do not. Frequently asked questions

Which cruise cabin is best for a first cruise?
There is no single answer. An inside or oceanview cabin keeps costs down if you will be out most of the day, while a balcony suits scenic itineraries. Check the deck plan for noise and location whichever you choose.
Is the cheapest cruise cabin always an inside cabin?
Usually, but not always. Fares vary by ship, sailing date and offers, and guaranteed cabins (where the line assigns your cabin) can be cheaper still. Compare what is included in each fare.
Where is the quietest place for a cruise cabin?
Cabins away from restaurants, theatres, the pool deck and engine areas tend to be quieter. Check the deck plan for what is above and below the cabin you are considering.
